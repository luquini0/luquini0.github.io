SPF, DKIM and DMARC, step by step

Three DNS records decide whether your email lands in the inbox and whether anyone can pretend to be your domain. Set them up in this order.

Before you start: list every service that sends email as your domain (your mailbox provider, newsletter tool, invoicing, CRM, website forms). Missing one is the most common reason legitimate mail starts failing.

What each one does

  • SPF lists which servers may send mail for your domain.
  • DKIM adds a cryptographic signature to each message, checked against a public key in your DNS.
  • DMARC says what receivers should do when SPF and DKIM fail in alignment with the visible From domain, and where to send reports.

Step 1: SPF

Publish one TXT record at the root of your domain (@). Use the exact value your providers document. For a single provider it looks like this (Google Workspace as the example):

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

With several senders, combine them in the same record: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sender2.example ~all. Rules that bite:

  • Only one SPF record per name. RFC 7208 does not permit multiple, and receivers return a permanent error if they find two.
  • SPF evaluation is limited to 10 DNS-querying terms (include, a, mx, ptr, exists, redirect). Too many includes breaks SPF.
  • ~all (soft fail) is a sensible start; -all (hard fail) once you are sure the list is complete.

Step 2: DKIM

  1. In your mail provider's admin console, generate a DKIM key for your domain. It gives you a selector and a value.
  2. Publish it in DNS at selector._domainkey.yourdomain.com as a TXT record (some providers give you a CNAME instead; publish exactly what they show).
  3. Back in the console, turn signing on.
  4. Repeat for every other service that sends as your domain; each has its own selector.

Step 3: DMARC, starting in monitor mode

Publish a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com

p=none only monitors. The rua address receives aggregate reports (XML, usually daily) from big receivers. Read them, with a report-analysis tool if you prefer, to find legitimate senders that still fail.

Step 4: tighten, gradually

  1. When every legitimate sender passes, move to p=quarantine (suspicious mail goes to spam).
  2. After a quiet period, move to p=reject (receivers refuse failing mail).
  3. Optional: the pct tag applies the policy to a percentage of mail while you ramp up.

How to test

dig +short TXT yourdomain.com
dig +short TXT selector._domainkey.yourdomain.com
dig +short TXT _dmarc.yourdomain.com

These show your SPF, DKIM and DMARC records in that order. Then send a message from your domain to a personal Gmail account, open the message menu, choose "Show original", and look for SPF: PASS, DKIM: PASS and DMARC: PASS. Test mail from every sending service, not only your mailbox.

Common mistakes

  • Two SPF records, or more than 10 lookups.
  • Forgetting a sender (newsletter, invoicing, forms) and then jumping to p=reject.
  • A DKIM key published but signing never switched on.
  • Domains you never send from: they can still be spoofed. A record like v=spf1 -all plus a DMARC policy says "no mail comes from here".

Checklist

  • I listed every service that sends as my domain.
  • One SPF record, under 10 lookups, covering all of them.
  • DKIM published and signing enabled for each sender.
  • DMARC at _dmarc with p=none and a report address.
  • I read the reports and fixed the legitimate failures.
  • I moved to quarantine, then reject, when it was safe.
  • Parked domains have a "no mail" setup.
